'Gaudi, to his credit, never gave up on his dream, but that's not usually how it goes. I mean, usually, it isn't a speeding bus that keeps the brown, pointy, weird church from getting built. Most of the time, it's just too difficult or too expensive, or too scary. It's only once you've stopped that you realize how hard it is to start again, so you force yourself not to want it. But it's always there, and until you finish it, it will always be (unfinished).' - How I Met Your Mother, Season 6 Episode 3

For a long time in my life, I have embraced certainty. I tend to make decisions that lead to predictable outcomes. My insecurity of uncertainty has clouded my logical thinking for as far as I can recall. I previously signed a full scholarship that carries with itself a bond lasting a total of 6 years when I entered NUS. I will be 25 by the time I graduate from NUS and I would have been 31 by the time the bond is fully served should I decide to do so. For a long time, I imagined I would remain in Singapore until 31, and then until the end. I never even began to entertain the possibility of initiating a bond buy-out and seeking career opportunities elsewhere. Some seniors and friends have urged me to consider this option before but I simply dismissed it back then, for the simple reason that I embrace certainty. I have long feared the uncertainty and unpredictability associated with leaving a place I am familiar with. (Sure I left China at the age of 15, but it was different back then. I came here with middle school batchmates and acquaintances I was more or less familiar with. This time, a decision of leaving will involve myself and myself only.)

I first took this idea seriously after my first round interview with Jump Trading. I completely screwed it up – or at least I thought I did – and I was reviewing all my options again. I realized that Citadel was really the best option I had, which prompted me to think: ‘So why not just go for it? Sure it will be in HK Office, but will I really dismiss it just because it is in an environment I am less familiar with? I have come this far, am I really willing to just throw it away? I have a friend who received so many competitive offers and in the end, chose Citadel last year. Am I really just going to throw away such a good opportunity to work at such a wonderful firm?’

It was at this point that I remembered the school motto from my former middle school back where I came from, Nanjing Foreign Language School, that says ‘Chinese Soul, Global Vision’. I guess I am never intended to be bound and rooted in one singular place after all. I guess I am supposed to be a ‘global citizen’ in some fashion. I have been in China Mainland for 15 years and I will be in Singapore for 10 by the time I graduate from NUS. Perhaps I should at least stay open to the idea of beginning my next chapter elsewhere. I never did take this option seriously for a very long time, until my performance in the first round interview for Jump Trading re-kindled this thought. Sure Jump’s recruiter got back to me mere 3 hours later and advanced me to the final round – which I sincerely appreciate – but the damage had been done and the thought had already become stuck in my mind.

After this, I have taken geographical location out of my consideration when sourcing for my final internship (which leads to full-time opportunities). I am no longer factoring that in when I evaluate a company as a choice of my 2022 summer internship destination. **I have not yet ruled out the possibility of staying in Singapore and serving out the bond upon my graduation, but if I do make such a decision, it will be a result of clear logical thinking rather than of fear and insecurity.**

Since this will be my last summer internship during my undergraduate candidature, naturally return offer is on my mind and I have done careful evaluations and analyses of all the companies that have offered me internship positions. I talked to the companies’ past interns and current employees. I took into account tech stacks, work culture as well as transparency in communication when I made my decision. I want to work in a meritocratic place where I am given the freedom and respect to demonstrate what I am capable of. I am grateful for all the companies that have offered me internship opportunities, but in the end, I am only able to choose one. **With the criteria listed above in mind, I have decided that in the next summer, I am going to take my talents to Hong Kong and join Citadel as an intern.**

I’d like to think Citadel is one of the most meritocratic places on this Earth, and I only ever want to be evaluated based on my technical strengths, my communication skills, and my work ethic. Citadel does not discriminate against candidates based on who they are or where they are from. I’d like to believe that its hiring strategy attracts the best talents available, and I look forward to being a part of it. I imagine I would be unbelievably happy on a daily basis to work alongside the best talents the world has ever seen.

Looking back, I have had an unbelievable undergraduate candidature these past three and a half years at NUS. I did Double Degree, two academic programmes of my choosing (one of which is arguably one of the most challenging academic programmes any university has to offer), and I survived; I did Teaching Assistance, tons of them; I did two internships and am about to do the last, out of the three one was with Goldman and another is with Citadel. I think the ‘under promise and over deliver’ thing really worked out for me. (I learnt this phrasing from my days at Goldman, but as it turns out, I have been doing this for quite a while.)

Prior to joining NUS, I was a nobody, holding a measly 6 distinctions from the A-Level days (out of a total of 8 possible ones). A lot of people never believed I would be able to improve so much and achieve so much in the university. To be fair at times I lost that belief myself. After all, I am still a work-in-progress that is imperfect in so many ways. I have by and large been an underdog for most of my life. I have made many failed attempts before delivering the final shot. Just the year before, I was rejected by Citadel at the coding round. I have also been shut out by Facebook two years in a row at resume screening with zero feedback. It is just so much easier to stay in my comfort zone and acknowledge defeat. After all, I don’t even have a Singapore PR, which makes it so much harder to compete for equal opportunities at top firms for the Singapore Office, let alone the fact that I possess not even one singular thing or attribute emphasized by equality hire or diversity hire. It is just so much easier to throw in the towel rather than to fight back. This has proved to be a no-win scenario for many other people, but not me. I guess Star Trek has taught me well. I don’t believe in no-win scenarios. I just study as hard as I possibly can, I just learn from the best of the best around me as much as possible, and I just see what happens. I believe that efforts do pay off if there exists any justice in this world. I believe that I am just as capable of getting into the top firms as many others despite my disadvantages as defined by the system. I am just betting that there are still some good firms out there that focus on the true calibre of the candidates and care what the candidates can deliver rather than what labels these candidates carry, and luckily there are. Most people thought that candidates go to hedge funds and high-frequency trading firms just because of money. Well, at least for me that is not the case. (Hell, Goldman already paid me more than I needed!) To me, they focus on bringing in the best talents, giving them the opportunities and resources to shine, and showing them the respect they deserve. A lot of people tend to criticize these firms for the lack of social utility. I guess we can have that discussion when the companies that are supposedly filled with social utility start to advance candidates past resume screening when the candidates study under double degree programme and possess first-class honours, 5-time teaching assistance experience, prior internship experience at Goldman as well as an internal referral.

Luckily my efforts meant something, and did lead to empirical results, which cannot be said in so many other cases. I do count myself lucky though, because there was always someone there for me that helped me steer through the turbulent times and the dark periods. I was never alone in my fight. Just to name a few (in the chronological sequence of our first encounter), Ruomu, my mentor when I was a freshie, stayed connected with me throughout the years and he just kept inspiring me to constantly improve and gave me the most useful advice at all the critical junctures; Prof Ben, for whom I worked for two iterations of CS2040S as a TA, referred me to my first two internships at Endowus and Goldman during the time of the pandemic; Bernard, taught me so much C++ and so many advanced algorithms, selflessly spent countless hours with me going over possible interview questions across all CS foundations as well as any kind of imperfections in my preparation work before my interviews and guided me even beyond the interviews and internships themselves… The list is a long one. I am immensely grateful for all the wonderful friends who kept believing in me, encouraging me and inspiring me. I will never be where I am today without you guys. May our paths cross again in the future, and all the best!
