I have recently been talking to a couple of friends who have completed their Computer Science (CS) education at the National University of Singapore (NUS), and we have shared our perspectives on the NUS CS education - or rather, what's bad or somewhat lacking about it. As it turns out, our perspectives do share certain commonality. While I will be sharing my opinions on the dark side of NUS CS education, I want to preface them by saying I am actually very grateful that I went through NUS CS education and for what I will be sharing subsequently, I mean them in the nicest and least offensive way possible.

One of the things I feel very strongly about NUS CS education, especially for all the lower level 1k and 2k modules, along with some of the higher level 3k and 4k modules, is that the modules are **not difficult, not intellectually stimulating, but just a little bit hard to score**. That's all. I actually gave a very vivid analogy for this when I tried to justify why this description is not self-contradictory. When I was in Junior Middle 3 (Sec 3 equivalent in Singapore) in Nanjing Foreign Language School and preparing for the High School Entrance Examination (O Level equivalent in Singapore), in the very last mathematics class, the teacher gave each student an A4 paper full of 3-digit additions/subtractions exercises (e.g. 123+456), and asked us to attempt them in 5 or 10 minutes or so. (I can't remember the exact timing, but it was something that most students can rush up just enough to finish in time with a bit of time pressure without a lot of time to slow down or double-check. I also feel obligated to explain at this point that in China, Mathematics examinations typically do not allow calculators, which is the whole point of this exercise - to test whether you can calculate accurately under time pressure.) As it turned out, actually only 3 or 4 students out of 60 got them all correct, if my memory serves. Most students made maybe 2 or 3 mistakes here and there.

Is this exercise difficult? Nope, not at all. Child's play for Sec 3 students.
Is it intellectually stimulating? Hell no!
Is it a bit hard to score? Yep, if you try to get full marks. And if you bell-curve everyone's grade, it is gonna be a disaster for those who made maybe just one more mistake than the median count.

Of course, that's actually the whole point of this test, purely to see whether you can be meticulous enough to avoid making careless mistakes under time pressure. Unfortunately, the reason I draw this comparison is that my feelings towards this exercise sort of resemble what I feel about the NUS CS education. I want to point out that I am **not** saying NUS CS is as easy as 3-digit additions & subtractions. Somehow nowadays those words are being put into my mouth ._.

What I am saying, however, is that in NUS CS, the content is not difficult enough and I think most cohort intakes nowadays, maybe more than 95% of them, can understand the lectures effortlessly. Given the intake threshold these days, I am not surprised. Most of the students can grasp the content being taught in lectures and tutorials and it is really not that hard so to speak. So the game is really just about practicing tons of past year papers and practice questions so that they could improve marginally on the final score and beat the others on the bell curve. Personally, I just think is a sub-optimal usage of my time and energy to practice a hell lot of questions, which does not really improve my understanding of the CS concepts involved themselves - I already understood them the time I sit for the lectures and then again the time I sit for the tutorials and/or recitations. All this sort of practicing can accomplish is to familiarise myself with the way the course materials could come out in the examinations and familiarising myself with this particular mode of testing so that I could perform marginally better in the examination settings. Sure it would probably give me a CAP boost of maybe 0.1 or 0.2, but really, what's the fun in that?

In the end, it is just about an endless cycle of involution. The concepts and materials being taught are not difficult with respect to the capability and maturity of the cohort, so the professors simply differentiate students by either increasing the workload in the exam papers while keeping the examination time constant (a good example would be CS2100), or by the number of careless mistakes made (a good example would be CS2105). This is just a depressing way of examining and differentiating the students in my opinion, which does not really reflect the students' mastery of the CS content itself. For me, I just really don't feel like being trapped in this endless cycle of involution. I don't practice that much. When I understand the concepts, that's it. I don't normally practice for the sake of practice. This is probably why I generally perform better in the 4k modules than the 2k modules, since there are usually no past year papers available in 4k modules. Even if they are, chances are they were under different professors and the examination styles differ greatly. When that happens, no one practice a lot and the examinations go back to test whether the students truly grasp the concepts or not, which plays in my favour a little bit because I tend to spend more time understanding the concepts themselves and I seem to be able to do that better than most others.

One interesting thing is that the current NUS CS syllabus is getting dumber and dumber, despite the cohort is getting stronger and stronger - well, supposedly at least. For instance, the Data Structures and Algorithms (now 2k modules, coded as CS2040/C/S) used to be covered in the 1k module known as CS1102 about 20 years ago. Apparently, in the CS1102 syllabus, they did cover rather advanced data structures like RBT which is no longer covered in the current syllabus. Thankfully we have CS2040S in Semester 2 nowadays - basically CS2020 a few years back - which is a great module with good algorithmic rigour. Unfortunately for the other variants, CS2040S in Semester 1 included, are just plainly and simply too easy.

Another example would be CS2106, where the syllabus 10 years ago covered more advanced concepts like deadlock detection, the Coffman's conditions for deadlock, and how to tackle any one of them to prevent deadlock. Instead, the syllabus nowadays simply teaches in a very magical and superficial manner. I remember when I took CS2106, there was a tutorial question that mentioned you can solve the Dining Philosophers problem if one of the philosophers have a different chirality than the others. E.g. all but one philosopher is left-handed / right-handed. What the syllabus has failed to teach is **why** this works. As it turns out, this solution is derivable from the Coffman's conditions. The way I see this, it is not just about trimming down on the content, but rather, trimming down core concepts that actually connect the concepts covered altogether, which are actually what make CS beautiful, elegant and fun.

Now that I have been tearing apart the 2k modules, I might as well continue to tear apart the 1k modules for completeness of my argument about the lower-level NUS CS modules in general. CS1231 for instance, I have no idea why Natural Deduction was taken out of the syllabus when it is obviously one of the most crucial and integral parts of the only Mathematics module in CS. (Yeah I know there are MA1101R/MA1102R/ST2334. I am referring to the discrete mathematics and logic part that is fundamental and relevant to every single domain in CS). I haven't taken CS3234, maybe ND has been put there instead, I am uncertain. But nevertheless, ND is important enough and deserves a place in CS1231. Another thing is the examination mode. I have no idea why there were only MCQs and writing down the final results type of questions for my batch in the final, when proof is obviously the most emphasized technique in this module. Just making them appear in the assignments is really bad in terms of the rigour of the module. For CS1010/CS1101S, actually I have no complaints there, but there have been too many complaints here and there from students taking other variants like CS1010S. I have no idea why students who have failed to apply themselves for an introductory module actually have the audacity to whine and complain on Reddit and NUSWhispers about the strict marking and the bell curve etc. etc. Where I come from, if you suck, it is your problem, not the teachers'. If you don't want to put in any effort at all to understand the things being taught in the first place - which are not even difficult, by the way - then what exactly gives you the courage to complain about the professors who bother to put in the effort to really teach the contents, pass those who deserve to pass and fail those who deserve to fail? I have interacted with students who have passed CS1010S before and tons of them cannot even write out a simple straightforward binary search subroutine. The bar is really not that high and this is just plainly depressing.

Interestingly, there seem to be more voices nowadays from the students claiming the modules are more difficult despite the students are supposedly getting stronger - the module are **NOT** difficult! They really aren't. I think it is just that students these days can access social media pretty easily, and for some reason, they do seem to influence the NUS School of Computing teaching teams a bit. Nowadays there are just questions streaming in from students always asking 'Why CSxxxx midterm/final/assignments so difficult, need help' on Reddit. And when they don't study enough, they also post on NUSWhispers asking 'Hey Prof Ben, look at the bell curve for CSxxxx. Any comments?' I think there is just too much whining and bitching going on these days. In the old days, when Reddit was not as popular and NUSWhispers was not present, whenever students faced difficulty, they probably just had to suck it up rather than 'ask Prof Ben' and whine about it on the Internet.

Another way to look at this is by comparing it with other majors, like Physics. I know, because I study both. I am one of the only two current undergraduate students who are able to make this comparison. I have talked to the other one, on why we do Physics. I mean we both really just need the CS degree for our jobs, and a more common and popular option for DDP is CS and Math. I think what we did agree in that discussion is that Physics - despite being more difficult - is much more fun. **It is not fun if it is easy**, you know. I don't identify with those who spend a hell lot of time doing past year papers for the marginal CAP boost. I feel time is much better spent on learning exciting new things. To be perfectly honest, I suck at Physics, and I suck even more when you use the other CS and Physics DDP student as the reference. To keep up with what's going on, I have to go to every single lecture and tutorial. And when I am not smart enough to understand the professors, I have to refer to the textbooks. I remember so much time, energy (and yes, my hair as well) has been consumed on reading through the Griffiths among other textbooks. And when I still cannot understand, I ask my friends, I ask the professors. I have to struggle just to be a student that is mediocre at best in Physics.

I remember when I was taking Math Method in Physics I under Kenneth, he talked about this. The Physics department was fully aware that Physics was not exactly very popular among the freshies. They were already thankful that the cohort size can still maintain at double digits. They were thinking of ways to 'promote' physics and try to get more students to take it as a major or even just as a second major or a minor. One of the intuitive methods was to trim down on the difficulty level and the module content being taught. They considered it at the time, and they rejected it, because one of their colleagues raised this query: If your child is studying physics at NUS, do you think his professor should omit these topics just because they are difficult? They realized what an absurd idea that was and they then maintained the difficulty as is, because despite these topics made the physics students' lives much, much tougher than their friends doing other majors, and also made the Physics major itself much, much less favourable among incoming undergraduates, the topics and contents are integral to undergraduate level physics education and the department just refused to back down. They made sure that the physics undergraduates receive the proper training they need for physics undergraduate education. (Of course, they lost the war later when CHS was born. Every single major previously under FOS has been crippled and deformed to the very core. No science major could have survived that holocaust when 5/8 of the curriculum has been filled with generic irrelevant modules and physics is now basically a hollowed out shell of a CHS major. The Physics Department was part of a small minority and **they don't stand a chance against the circus when the circus comes to town. They are overmatched.** But hey, at least they tried to stand for and fought for what they believed in, and I respect them for it.)

Physics is certainly difficult, but on the other hand, it gives me a purpose and makes my university life much less dull. So, to put things into perspective, I think one issue with NUS CS is that it is not rigorous enough to generate those intellectually stimulating moments. There are only rare moments when I feel certain topics being taught are particularly fascinating and are actually intellectually stimulating enough. In this past year, I can probably only recall 2 fascinating moments when I study CS in NUS - one is when I do the Queueing Theory in CS4226, where I finally get some mathematical rigour comparable to 2k Physics module; the other is when I do the Normal Forms in CS2102, where I tasted some basic Group Theory inside (I am uncertain how and the possible connections in between, but I did get that feeling). So I think NUS CS lacks a bit of challenge in terms of rigour from my point of view.

Call me orthodox, or call me pedantic, but I honestly feel that NUS CS can get better in terms of its academic rigour. Unfortunately, with the new reformation going on at the university level, it seems every major, every degree is on the chopping block. More common modules and breadth modules really dumb down the difficulty of NUS CS and its prestige along with it in my honest opinion. I miss the old days - I think it was pre-AY2017/18? - when the NUS CS curriculum had only 24 UEs and everything else was CS-related. Personally, for NUS CS students, this era is probably the best for aspiring software engineers, but probably the worst for aspiring computer scientists.
